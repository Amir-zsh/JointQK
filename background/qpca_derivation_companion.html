    An Interactive Companion

    Optimal Low-Rank Reconstruction for Inner-Product Preservation

    A closed-form derivation, with formal results and interpretive remarks

    ⁂

    Topic · Source coding · Query-weighted PCA · KV-cache compression

    Mathematical level · Linear algebra, convex optimization, projection geometry

    Connections · Linder–Zamir–Zeger (1999) · Fan (1949)

    Empirical test · KV-cache compression on Qwen3-8B (§8–§9)

    We derive the closed-form solution of an all-pairs inner-product reconstruction objective involving two variables. Although the construction is stated as a dimensionality-reduction problem, the resulting linear transform is also the optimal decorrelating transform for quantization, as proven by Linder, Zamir, and Zeger via information-theoretic arguments. Section 8 reports an empirical test against the production-deployed Joint-QK heuristic basis: QPCA achieves its theoretical optimality on logit MSE (16–36 % reductions across bit widths) but loses to the heuristic on top-k attention argmax retention by 3–5 percentage points — a clean separation of two metrics that practitioners often conflate. Section 9 explains why.
    Intended audience PhD researchers, faculty, and technical founders working on quantization, neural compression, or KV-cache acceleration. We assume familiarity with eigendecompositions, projection operators, and basic convex optimization, but no prior background in source coding theory.
    Section 1

    Setup: the all-pairs reconstruction problem

    We are given a finite set of queries and keys in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Our task is to compress each key into a low-dimensional code so that, after decoding through a shared linear map, every query-key inner product is approximately preserved.

    Definition 1.1 — Data, encoder, decoder

    Fix integers $N, M, d, p$ with $p \le d$. Let $$\{q_i\}_{i=1}^N \subset \mathbb{R}^d, \qquad \{k_j\}_{j=1}^M \subset \mathbb{R}^d$$ denote the queries and keys. A rank-$p$ linear codec is the pair $$\bigl(\, U,\; \{r_j\}_{j=1}^M \,\bigr), \qquad U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}, \quad r_j \in \mathbb{R}^p,$$ where $U$ is a shared decoder applied to each per-key code $r_j$.

    The goal is to choose $U$ and $\{r_j\}$ so that $q_i^\top U r_j \approx q_i^\top k_j$ for every $(i, j)$. We measure fidelity by the squared error summed over all pairs:

    Objective 1.2 — All-pairs reconstruction loss

    $$L(U,\, \{r_j\}) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^M \bigl(\, q_i^\top k_j \;-\; q_i^\top U r_j \,\bigr)^2. \tag{1}$$

    Remark 1.3 — Why all-pairs, not all-keys

    The naïve fix is to minimise $\sum_j \|k_j - U r_j\|_2^2$ and call it done. That is the wrong loss: it weights every coordinate of $k_j$ equally, ignoring the fact that downstream consumers see keys only through inner products with queries. The all-pairs loss in (1) is the geometrically faithful objective when $q$'s are the only way keys ever enter computation — exactly the setting in attention layers, retrieval, and content-addressable memories.

    Remark 1.4 — Why this is non-obvious

    The objective (1) couples the optimisation over $U$ and $\{r_j\}$ across two summations ($N \cdot M$ terms), and it is quadratic in $U$ but biquadratic in $(U, \{r_j\})$ jointly. A priori there is no reason to expect a clean closed-form maximizer. The derivation that follows uncovers one — and shows it coincides with the optimal decorrelating transform from a separate information-theoretic argument.

    Section 2 · Step 1

    Reordering to expose the query covariance

    The objective (1) hides a useful structure: the sum over queries can be folded into a single matrix, the query second-moment matrix. Once we expose it, the problem decouples across keys.

    The algebraic step

    Group the terms inside each square:

    $$q_i^\top k_j \;-\; q_i^\top U r_j \;=\; q_i^\top \bigl( k_j - U r_j \bigr) \;=\; q_i^\top \delta_j, \qquad \delta_j \;:=\; k_j - U r_j.$$

    Then

    $$L \;=\; \sum_{i,j} \bigl(q_i^\top \delta_j\bigr)^2 \;=\; \sum_{i,j} \delta_j^\top q_i q_i^\top \delta_j \;=\; \sum_j \delta_j^\top \!\left( \sum_i q_i q_i^\top \right)\! \delta_j.$$
    Definition 2.1 — Query second-moment matrix

    $$M_q \;\doteq\; \sum_{i=1}^N q_i q_i^\top \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}.$$ $M_q$ is symmetric positive semidefinite. We assume throughout that $M_q$ is invertible (equivalently, $M_q \succ 0$); a necessary condition is $N \ge d$ and the queries span $\mathbb{R}^d$.

    Lemma 2.2 — Equivalent objective

    With $\delta_j := k_j - U r_j$ and $M_q$ as above, $$L(U,\,\{r_j\}) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^M \bigl(k_j - U r_j\bigr)^{\!\top} M_q \bigl(k_j - U r_j\bigr). \tag{2}$$

    Proof sketch

    Expanding $(q_i^\top \delta_j)^2 = \delta_j^\top q_i q_i^\top \delta_j$ and exchanging the order of summation gives $L = \sum_j \delta_j^\top M_q \delta_j$. Substituting $\delta_j = k_j - Ur_j$ yields (2).

    ▢

    Remark 2.3 — From all-pairs to a weighted regression

    The objective (2) is recognisable: it is a sum of $M_q$-weighted Euclidean errors on key reconstructions. The query distribution has been distilled into a single matrix; the keys no longer interact with individual $q_i$'s. This is the linchpin of everything that follows.

    Remark 2.4 — Why invertibility matters

    The Mahalanobis-style weighting $\delta^\top M_q \delta$ is a proper norm only when $M_q \succ 0$. If $M_q$ has a non-trivial null space (e.g., when the queries lie in a strict subspace), errors along null directions are invisible to the loss and the optimum is non-unique. In practice, when $N$ is large and queries are diverse — as in attention layers across many tokens — $M_q$ is well-conditioned; in pathological corners (layer 0 with attention sinks, very short prefixes), explicit regularisation $M_q \mapsto M_q + \varepsilon I$ is prudent.

    Section 3 · Step 2

    Optimal codes for a fixed decoder

    Holding $U$ fixed, the objective decouples across keys. Each $r_j^*$ is a generalised least-squares projection of $k_j$ onto the column space of $U$ — measured in the $M_q$ inner product.

    Proposition 3.1 — Closed-form optimal codes

    Fix $U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}$ of full column rank. The objective (2) decouples as $L = \sum_j L_j(r_j)$ with $L_j(r_j) = (k_j - Ur_j)^\top M_q (k_j - Ur_j)$. The unique minimiser in each $r_j$ is $$r_j^* \;=\; \bigl(U^\top M_q U\bigr)^{-1} U^\top M_q\, k_j. \tag{3}$$

    Proof sketch

    $L_j$ is a strictly convex quadratic in $r_j$ (since $U^\top M_q U \succ 0$ when $U$ has full column rank). The gradient is

    $$\nabla_{r_j} L_j(r_j) \;=\; -2 U^\top M_q (k_j - U r_j).$$

    Setting it to zero gives the normal equations $U^\top M_q U \, r_j = U^\top M_q k_j$. Invertibility of $U^\top M_q U$ then yields (3). ▢

    Remark 3.2 — Geometric meaning

    Formula (3) is the $M_q$-orthogonal projection of $k_j$ onto $\mathrm{col}(U)$. If we change the inner product on $\mathbb{R}^d$ from the standard Euclidean one to $\langle x, y\rangle_{M_q} := x^\top M_q y$, then the operator $U(U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q$ is the orthogonal projector for that inner product. The same construction is called oblique projection in the Euclidean sense and generalised least squares in statistics.

    Remark 3.3 — Why this matters for what follows

    Eliminating $r_j$ in favour of $U$ is the standard EM-style trick: we have reduced a joint optimisation in $(U, \{r_j\})$ to a single optimisation in $U$ alone. The next step substitutes (3) back into (2) and confronts the remaining problem head-on.

    Remark 3.4 — When the inverse exists

    $U^\top M_q U \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ is invertible iff $U$ has full column rank and $M_q \succ 0$. The second is our standing assumption; the first will be automatic at the optimum, since we will pick $U$ to be a slice of an orthogonal basis after a change of variables.

    Section 4 · Step 3 (Part I)

    Optimal decoder: the oblique projector

    Substituting (3) back into (2) eliminates the codes. The residual takes a particularly clean form once we identify the matrix $P_U$ as a (Mahalanobis-)projector. Two technical lemmas establish its properties; an identity for the residual then reduces the loss to a trace expression that depends on $U$ only.

    The oblique projector

    Substituting $r_j^*$ from (3) gives

    $$U r_j^* \;=\; U(U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q\, k_j \;=:\; P_U \, k_j.$$
    Definition 4.1 — Oblique projector $P_U$

    $$P_U \;:=\; U\bigl(U^\top M_q U\bigr)^{-1} U^\top M_q \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}.$$ $P_U$ projects onto $\mathrm{col}(U)$ along the $M_q$-orthogonal complement of $\mathrm{col}(U)$. It is the natural projector when $M_q$ defines the metric.

    Lemma 4.2 — Idempotency

    $P_U^2 = P_U$.

    Proof

    By direct substitution and cancellation of the inner $(U^\top M_q U)$ pair:

    $$P_U^2 \;=\; U(U^\top M_q U)^{-1} \underbrace{U^\top M_q U}_{\text{cancels}} (U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q \;=\; U(U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q \;=\; P_U. \quad\square$$
    Lemma 4.3 — $M_q P_U$ is symmetric

    $(M_q P_U)^\top = M_q P_U$, equivalently $M_q P_U = P_U^\top M_q$.

    Proof

    Write $A := M_q U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}$ and $B := U^\top M_q U = A^\top U$. Since $M_q$ is symmetric, $A^\top = U^\top M_q$. Then

    $$M_q P_U \;=\; M_q U (U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q \;=\; A \, B^{-1} A^\top.$$

    Since $B$ is symmetric so is $B^{-1}$, and $AB^{-1}A^\top$ is manifestly symmetric. $\square$

    The residual identity

    The reconstruction error is $k_j - U r_j^* = (I - P_U)\, k_j$, and the loss becomes

    $$L^*(U) \;=\; \sum_{j} k_j^\top (I - P_U)^\top M_q (I - P_U)\, k_j.$$
    Proposition 4.4 — Residual identity

    $$(I - P_U)^\top M_q (I - P_U) \;=\; M_q - M_q P_U.$$

    Proof

    Expand the left side, using $P_U^\top M_q = M_q P_U$ (Lemma 4.3) and $P_U^2 = P_U$ (Lemma 4.2):

    $$(I - P_U)^\top M_q (I - P_U) \;=\; M_q \;-\; M_q P_U \;-\; \underbrace{P_U^\top M_q}_{= M_q P_U} \;+\; \underbrace{P_U^\top M_q P_U}_{= M_q P_U^2 = M_q P_U}.$$

    The middle terms collapse, leaving $M_q - M_q P_U$. $\square$

    Remark 4.5 — The trace reformulation

    Defining the key second-moment matrix $S_k := \sum_j k_j k_j^\top$ and using the identity $\sum_j k_j^\top A k_j = \mathrm{tr}(A S_k)$, the loss becomes $$L^*(U) \;=\; \mathrm{tr}\!\bigl(M_q S_k\bigr) \;-\; \mathrm{tr}\!\bigl(M_q P_U S_k\bigr).$$ The first term does not depend on $U$, so we may equivalently maximise

    $$T(U) \;\doteq\; \mathrm{tr}\!\bigl( M_q U (U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q S_k \bigr). \tag{4}$$

    Remark 4.6 — Why this rewrite is the right one

    The maximisation problem (4) is still non-convex in $U$ — the term $(U^\top M_q U)^{-1}$ is non-polynomial. The next step uses a clever change of variables to transmute (4) into a familiar PCA problem with a known closed-form solution.

    Section 5 · Step 3 (Part II)

    Change of variables: the problem becomes PCA

    A single substitution converts the non-convex trace objective (4) into the classical Rayleigh quotient maximisation — a problem solved a century ago by Fan and others. The substitution is geometric: rescale the ambient space by $M_q^{1/2}$ so that the $M_q$-inner product becomes Euclidean.

    The substitution

    Since $M_q \succ 0$, its positive symmetric square root $M_q^{1/2}$ is well-defined. Let

    $$V \;:=\; M_q^{1/2}\, U \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}.$$

    Then $U = M_q^{-1/2} V$, and elementary algebra gives

    $$U^\top M_q U \;=\; V^\top V, \qquad M_q U \;=\; M_q^{1/2} V.$$

    Substituting into (4):

    $$T \;=\; \mathrm{tr}\!\Bigl( M_q^{1/2} V (V^\top V)^{-1} V^\top M_q^{1/2} S_k \Bigr) \;=\; \mathrm{tr}(\Pi_V \, A),$$

    where the cyclic property of trace and the following definitions are used:

    Definition 5.1 — Reduced problem

    $$A \;:=\; M_q^{1/2}\, S_k\, M_q^{1/2}, \qquad \Pi_V \;:=\; V (V^\top V)^{-1} V^\top.$$ $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is symmetric positive semidefinite (a congruence of $S_k$). $\Pi_V$ is the standard Euclidean orthogonal projector of rank $p$ onto $\mathrm{col}(V)$.

    The maximisation (4) has reduced to:

    $$\boxed{\quad \text{Maximise}\;\; \mathrm{tr}(\Pi_V \, A) \;\;\text{over rank-}p\text{ orthogonal projectors }\Pi_V. \quad}$$
    Theorem 5.2 — Fan (1949) [2]

    Maximal trace of $\Pi A$ over rank-$p$ projectors

    Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be symmetric with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_d$ and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, \ldots, v_d$. Among all rank-$p$ orthogonal projectors $\Pi$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$, $$\max_\Pi\, \mathrm{tr}(\Pi A) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^p \lambda_i,$$ and the maximum is attained by $\Pi^* = V_p V_p^\top$ where $V_p := [v_1, \ldots, v_p]$.

    Remark 5.3 — This is principal component analysis

    Theorem 5.2 is the trace-form statement of PCA. To preserve the largest amount of variance (or — equivalently here — to maximise the projected trace of $A$) one chooses the top-$p$ eigenvectors. The novelty in the present derivation is not Theorem 5.2 itself but the chain of transformations that brings (1) into a form where Fan's theorem applies.

    Remark 5.4 — Why $M_q^{1/2}$ is the right rescaling

    The matrix $M_q^{1/2}$ is the unique positive symmetric matrix that turns the bilinear form $\langle x, y\rangle_{M_q} = x^\top M_q y$ into the standard Euclidean inner product $\langle x, y\rangle = (M_q^{1/2} x)^\top (M_q^{1/2} y)$. Under this rescaling, $M_q$-orthogonal projectors become Euclidean orthogonal projectors — and the non-convex optimisation in (4) becomes the convex problem in Theorem 5.2.

    Section 6 · Main result

    Closed-form optimum

    Assembling Sections 1 through 5 yields a closed-form solution to the original all-pairs reconstruction problem (1). We state the main theorem.

    Theorem 6.1 — Optimal rank-$p$ codec

    Closed-form minimiser of the all-pairs reconstruction loss

    Assume $M_q := \sum_i q_i q_i^\top$ is positive definite. Let $$A \;=\; M_q^{1/2}\, S_k\, M_q^{1/2}, \qquad S_k \;:=\; \sum_{j} k_j k_j^\top,$$ and let $A = V \Lambda V^\top$ be its eigendecomposition with $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_d)$. Denote by $V_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times p}$ the matrix of top-$p$ eigenvectors. Then the rank-$p$ codec $$\boxed{\; U^* \;=\; M_q^{-1/2} V_p, \qquad r_j^* \;=\; V_p^\top M_q^{1/2}\, k_j \;}$$ minimises (1), and the optimal loss is $$L^*(U^*) \;=\; \mathrm{tr}(M_q S_k) \;-\; \sum_{i=1}^{p} \lambda_i.$$

    Proof — assembling the chain

    Lemma 2.2 reduces (1) to (2). Proposition 3.1 minimises (2) in $\{r_j\}$ for fixed $U$ and produces (3). Substituting (3) into (2) and applying Lemmas 4.2–4.3 and Proposition 4.4 yields

    $$L^*(U) \;=\; \mathrm{tr}(M_q S_k) - \mathrm{tr}\bigl(M_q U(U^\top M_q U)^{-1} U^\top M_q S_k\bigr).$$

    The substitution $V := M_q^{1/2} U$ reduces the second trace to $\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_V A)$. Theorem 5.2 (Fan) gives the maximiser $\Pi_V^* = V_p V_p^\top$ with maximum $\sum_{i=1}^p \lambda_i$. The optimal $U^*$ recovers via $U^* = M_q^{-1/2} V_p$. The optimal codes follow by re-substitution into (3): since $U^{*\top} M_q U^* = V_p^\top V_p = I_p$,

    $$r_j^* \;=\; (U^{*\top} M_q U^*)^{-1} U^{*\top} M_q k_j \;=\; V_p^\top M_q^{1/2} k_j. \quad \square$$

    Corollary 6.2 — Code-space second moment

    Under the optimal encoder $r_j^* = V_p^\top M_q^{1/2} k_j$, $$\sum_j r_j^* r_j^{*\top} \;=\; V_p^\top A V_p \;=\; \Lambda_p \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{p \times p},$$ where $\Lambda_p = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_p)$. The optimal codes are uncorrelated, with coordinate $i$ carrying variance exactly $\lambda_i$.

    Corollary 6.3 — Full-rank exact reconstruction

    At $p = d$ (no rank reduction), $V_p = V$ is orthogonal, so $V V^\top = I$ and $U^* r_j^* = M_q^{-1/2} V V^\top M_q^{1/2} k_j = k_j$. The optimal codec reconstructs every key exactly in the absence of quantisation noise.

    Remark 6.4 — Q-weighted error equals unweighted code error

    Suppose codes are quantised: $\hat r_j = r_j^* + \eta_j$. Then $\hat k_j - k_j = U^* \eta_j = M_q^{-1/2} V_p \eta_j$. The $M_q$-weighted key error is $$\bigl(\hat k_j - k_j\bigr)^{\!\top} M_q \bigl(\hat k_j - k_j\bigr) \;=\; \eta_j^\top V_p^\top M_q^{-1/2} M_q M_q^{-1/2} V_p \eta_j \;=\; \eta_j^\top V_p^\top V_p \eta_j \;=\; \|\eta_j\|_2^2.$$ That is: the $M_q$-weighted key MSE is the plain Euclidean code MSE. The full geometry of the query weighting has been absorbed into the basis. This is the structural reason QPCA is the right basis for downstream quantisation: bit-allocation in code space can ignore $M_q$ entirely.

    Section 7 · Implementation

    The procedure in practice

    Theorem 6.1 yields a three-step recipe. Each step uses standard linear-algebra primitives; the practitioner needs only an SPD square-root routine and a symmetric eigensolver.

    Procedure 7.1 — Building the optimal codec

    Given $\{q_i\}_{i=1}^N$ and $\{k_j\}_{j=1}^M$:

    1. Form the second-moment matrices $M_q = \sum_i q_i q_i^\top$ and $S_k = \sum_j k_j k_j^\top$.
    2. Eigendecompose $A = M_q^{1/2} S_k M_q^{1/2}$; collect eigenvectors into $V$, sorted by descending eigenvalue.
    3. Set $U^* = M_q^{-1/2} V$ and encode each key as $r_j^* = V^\top M_q^{1/2} k_j$.

    For quantisation: encode each key, quantise the coefficients $r_j^*$ (uniformly, or by Lloyd–Max, etc.), and decode through $U^*$.

    Special cases

    Remark 7.2 — When $M_q \propto I$, this is plain PCA on keys

    If the queries are isotropic (uncorrelated unit variance), then $M_q = c I$ and $A = c \cdot S_k$. The eigenvectors of $A$ coincide with those of $S_k$, and the procedure becomes ordinary PCA on the key set. The query distribution adds geometric information only when it is anisotropic.

    Remark 7.3 — Non-orthonormal columns

    The optimal decoder $U^*$ does not have orthonormal columns in the standard Euclidean sense. It has $M_q$-orthonormal columns: $U^{*\top} M_q U^* = I_p$. Forcing $U^\top U = I$ in addition is a strictly more constrained problem that admits no closed form.

    Remark 7.4 — Connection to source coding

    Linder, Zamir, and Zeger [1] showed via a separate, information-theoretic argument that this same transform $U^*$ is the optimal decorrelating transform for high-resolution source coding under any non-difference distortion measure that is locally quadratic with $M_q$ as its curvature. The closed-form derivation here is the geometric counterpart of their entropy-driven optimality argument.

    Remark 7.5 — Numerical considerations

    The pipeline involves $M_q^{1/2}$ and $M_q^{-1/2}$, both formed via the spectral square root. If $M_q$ has condition number $\kappa$, then $A$'s condition number is bounded by $\kappa \cdot \kappa(S_k)$, which can be large. In practice it is wise to (i) form $M_q^{\pm 1/2}$ via symmetric eigendecomposition in double precision, (ii) symmetrise intermediate products (e.g., $A \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(A + A^\top)$), and (iii) regularise $M_q$ along directions with small eigenvalues by adding $\varepsilon \cdot \mathrm{tr}(M_q)/d \cdot I$.

    Section 8 · Empirical evaluation

    Does QPCA beat the heuristic on real attention?

    Theorem 6.1 asserts QPCA is the closed-form minimiser of the all-pairs logit reconstruction loss. We test the practical question directly: applied to KV-cache compression in a real transformer, does QPCA outperform the heuristic Joint-QK eigenbasis used in production?

    Setup

    Protocol

    Model: Qwen3-8B (36 layers × 32 query heads × 8 KV heads, head dimension 128). Calibration corpus: 450 LongBench train prompts spanning nine tasks (the project's "compact9" pool — hotpotqa, multi_news, musique, passage_retrieval_en, qasper, qmsum, repobench-p, triviaqa, lcc). Evaluation: 90 held-out test prompts (10 per task). Per-(layer, KV-head) second moments $M_q$ and $S_k$ are pooled across all train tokens and used to build the basis.

    We compare three K-side bases at $b \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ average bits per coordinate, with continuous water-fill bit allocation capped at 8 bits/coord:

    • v3 — random Hadamard rotation (TurboQuant baseline, calibration-independent).
    • jointqk — eigenvectors of $\tfrac{1}{2}(M_q S_k + S_k M_q)$, water-fill score $\mathrm{diag}(R^\top M_q R) \cdot \mathrm{diag}(R^\top S_k R)$. The production basis: a heuristic symmetrization, not derived from any objective.
    • qpca — the Theorem 6.1 basis $U^* = M_q^{-1/2} V$, water-fill on $\Lambda$ alone (the Q-weighting is absorbed by the basis, per Remark 6.4).

    Numerics: $M_q^{\pm 1/2}$ via spectral square root in fp64, cast to fp32 for compression; $M_q$ regularised by $\mathrm{tr}(M_q)/d \cdot \varepsilon I$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$ (Remark 7.5). Quantizer: per-coordinate Lloyd–Max with unit-Gaussian centroids scaled by per-coord standard deviation.

    Correctness gates (run at startup, all passed)

    Before scoring, three sanity checks on the QPCA implementation:

    • $\lVert FG - I\rVert_F \le 2.45 \times 10^{-6}$ per (L, H) in fp32 (encoder–decoder are exact inverses).
    • Empirical code variance within $\sim 1.1\times$ of $\Lambda$ on log-scale (sample variance on a single test row vs train-pooled $\Lambda$).
    • $b = 8$ Lloyd–Max roundtrip relative MSE $= 6.2 \times 10^{-5}$ (Lloyd–Max at 8 bits is near-continuous; this confirms the high-resolution limit reproduces exact reconstruction).

    Observation 8.1 — QPCA achieves its design objective

    QPCA reduces the empirical logit reconstruction error vs the heuristic by

    • $-16\%$ at $b = 2$ ($2.52 \times 10^1$ vs $2.99 \times 10^1$)
    • $-22\%$ at $b = 3$ ($7.72$ vs $9.85$)
    • $-36\%$ at $b = 4$ ($2.39$ vs $3.72$)

    The improvement is largest at the highest bit budget, consistent with the Linder–Zamir–Zeger high-resolution asymptote (Remark 7.4): QPCA is optimal in the high-rate limit.

    Observation 8.2 — But top-1 retention drops

    On top-1 attention retention — the probability that $\arg\max_t (q^\top k_t)$ survives quantisation — QPCA underperforms by 3 to 5 percentage points at every bit width:

    • $-4.5$ pp at $b = 2$ (0.612 vs 0.657)
    • $-3.7$ pp at $b = 3$ (0.746 vs 0.783)
    • $-2.9$ pp at $b = 4$ (0.830 vs 0.859)

    This is the metric the downstream benchmark actually cares about, since it directly bounds agreement between the compressed and original attention maps. Both observations are robust; both deserve explanation. Section 9 takes them up.

    Section 9 · Interpretation

    Why MSE optimality doesn't imply argmax optimality

    QPCA dominates every MSE-flavoured metric in §8 yet loses on attention argmax retention by 3 to 5 percentage points. The gap is not an implementation artefact and not a marginal effect — it is the signature of three independent structural facts. We label them M1 (threshold metric), M2 (Linder–Zamir–Zeger regime), and M3 (bit allocation). Each contributes; the three compound.

    M1 — Argmax is a threshold statistic

    Observation 9.1 — The margin condition

    Fix a query $q$ and write true logits $\ell_t := q^\top k_t$, top-1 index $t^* := \arg\max_t \ell_t$, and per-key perturbation $\Delta\ell_t := q^\top(\hat k_t - k_t)$. The compressed argmax equals the original iff $$\Delta\ell_{t^*} - \Delta\ell_t \;>\; \ell_t - \ell_{t^*} \;=\; -\,m(t^*, t) \qquad \forall\, t \neq t^*,$$ where $m(t^*, t) := \ell_{t^*} - \ell_t > 0$ is the original margin. Under the working approximation $\Delta\ell_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_t^2)$, $$\mathbb{P}\bigl[\,\text{argmax preserved}\,\bigr] \;\approx\; \prod_{t \neq t^*}\, \Phi\!\left(\, \frac{m(t^*, t)}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_{t^*}^2}} \,\right).$$

    Remark 9.1.1 — Why MSE cannot bound argmax survival

    The marginal MSE $\sum_t \sigma_t^2$ enters the survival formula only through individual $\sigma_t^2$'s — never through the joint distribution of $(m_t, \sigma_t)$. Two estimators with identical MSE can differ drastically in argmax survival depending on whether perturbation variance lands on small-margin pairs or on the safe bulk. Argmax preservation is a covering property of the random vector $(\Delta\ell_t)_t$; MSE is a single moment of its marginals.

    M2 — The Linder–Zamir–Zeger guarantee doesn't extend

    Observation 9.2 — Scope of the LZZ optimality

    The Linder–Zamir–Zeger result invoked in Remark 7.4 (ref [1]) proves QPCA is the optimal high-resolution transform for distortion measures of the form $$d(k, \hat k) \;=\; (k - \hat k)^\top M(k)\, (k - \hat k) \;+\; o\!\bigl(\|k - \hat k\|^2\bigr),$$ with $M(\cdot)$ a smooth field of positive-definite matrices. The argmax-survival indicator $$\mathbb{1}\bigl\{\, \arg\max_t q^\top \hat k_t \;=\; \arg\max_t q^\top k_t \,\bigr\}$$ admits no such expansion: it is a $\{0, 1\}$-valued functional with a sharp threshold at the margin. QPCA inherits no optimality from LZZ on this metric.

    Remark 9.2.1 — High resolution does not rescue argmax

    The natural retort is "LZZ is asymptotic in rate; perhaps the gap closes at $b = 4$." It does not. The table in §8 shows QPCA's $k\_\mathrm{mse}$ at $b = 4$ is two orders of magnitude smaller than at $b = 2$, yet ~14% of argmaxes still flip. The threshold doesn't soften with resolution — it just gets attacked with a smaller noise budget. The asymptotic decoupling between MSE and argmax is a regime-mismatch issue, not a finite-rate one.

    M3 — Bit allocation: equal-error vs. logit-variance

    Observation 9.3 — Two water-fill rules

    After Lloyd–Max quantisation at $B_i$ bits per coordinate, per-coord noise variance is $\mathrm{Var}(\eta_i) \approx c\,\lambda_i \cdot 2^{-2 B_i}$ where $\lambda_i$ is the source variance on coord $i$. Subject to $\sum_i B_i = B_\text{tot}$:

    • QPCA: by Corollary 6.4 (the Q-weighted-equals-Euclidean identity), the Q-weighted key MSE in code space is $\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(\eta_i)$ — each coord contributes equally to the loss. Source variance per coord is the QPCA eigenvalue $\lambda_i$. Water-fill on $\lambda_i$ alone: $$B_i^{\text{QPCA}} \;\propto\; \tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 \lambda_i \;+\; \text{const}.$$
    • JointQK: in the orthogonal basis $R$, write $q_R := R^\top q,\, k_R := R^\top k$. Under a queries–keys independence approximation, the per-coord contribution to logit variance $\mathrm{Var}_{q, k}(\ell)$ factorises: $$\mathrm{Var}\!\bigl(q_R^{(i)} \cdot k_R^{(i)}\bigr) \;=\; \underbrace{(R^\top M_q R)_{ii}}_{q\_\text{diag}_i} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{(R^\top S_k R)_{ii}}_{k\_\text{diag}_i}.$$ Water-fill on this product: $$B_i^{\text{JointQK}} \;\propto\; \tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 \bigl(q\_\text{diag}_i \cdot k\_\text{diag}_i\bigr) \;+\; \text{const}.$$
    Remark 9.3.1 — What each rule equalises

    QPCA equalises the per-coord MSE in code space — the right thing for the all-pairs MSE objective by construction; the LZZ-style optimum. JointQK equalises the per-coord ratio of quantisation noise to logit variance. Coordinates that carry a large fraction of the logit's variability — and therefore a large fraction of its Shannon information about which key wins — receive proportionally more bits under JointQK. The argmax is determined by such coordinates, so JointQK protects the argmax through allocation alone, even though its basis is only a heuristic.

    The two rules coincide only when $q\_\text{diag}_i / \lambda_i$ is constant across $i$ — generically false on real attention layers.

    Two ancillary mechanisms

    Remark 9.4 — Heavy-tailed key distributions

    In transformer attention, keys are heavy-tailed: a small fraction of tokens (attention sinks, BOS/EOS, high-norm anchors, syntactic heads) holds most of the top-1 attention mass. The matrices $\Sigma_K, S_k$ are dominated by these outliers, so eigenvectors of $A = M_q^{1/2} S_k M_q^{1/2}$ are correctly pulled toward the heavy-tail subspace — QPCA's basis alignment is fine. The mismatch is at the bit-allocation level (Observation 9.3): within the chosen basis, QPCA spreads bits uniformly across high-$\lambda$ directions, whereas the argmax is sustained by an even smaller subset where the rare top-margin keys actually reside.

    Remark 9.5 — Non-orthogonal encoder × scalar Lloyd–Max

    A secondary effect: QPCA's encoder $F = M_q^{1/2} V$ is non-orthogonal in the standard Euclidean sense (it is $M_q$-orthonormal — Remark 7.3). The joint code distribution can be sheared in directions a per-coordinate Lloyd–Max quantiser cannot capture. JointQK's $R$ is Euclidean-orthogonal, so each code coordinate is closer to marginal Gaussian and matches the scalar quantiser more cleanly. A vector quantiser would close most of this gap; we did not test it.

    Sanity check on the diagnosis

    Observation 9.6 — The k_mse / logit_err split is the smoking gun

    Two metrics in the §8 table tell a clean story when read together:

    • $\mathrm{logit\_err} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q, k}\bigl[(q^\top(k - \hat k))^2\bigr]$ is, up to a normalisation, exactly the $M_q$-weighted key MSE that QPCA minimises by construction. QPCA wins this by 16–36% across bit widths.
    • $k\_\mathrm{mse} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_k\bigl[\|k - \hat k\|^2\bigr]$ is the unweighted key MSE — what plain PCA minimises. Neither QPCA nor JointQK targets it. The two methods are statistically tied (within 1–2%, exact tie at $b = 4$).

    Together these two facts rule out implementation error: QPCA is doing precisely what the derivation in §1–§7 promises, and JointQK is doing precisely what its heuristic motivates — neither more, neither less. The argmax deficit is therefore a loss-mismatch phenomenon, not a basis-construction error.

    Synthesis

    M1 explains why MSE and argmax can disagree at all — threshold vs. quadratic. M2 explains why the closed-form guarantee doesn't transfer — LZZ's regime is locally quadratic, argmax isn't. M3 explains the direction of the empirical gap — JointQK's allocation tracks logit variance, which carries argmax information. The three mechanisms are mathematically independent; together they account for the full 3–5 pp deficit.

    Conclusion 9.7 — Implications

    For deployment, and for theory

    Deployment: the production K-side basis stays at jointqk. QPCA's theoretical optimality is now a tested fact rather than a hope — it is optimality for an objective that disagrees with the application's metric.

    Theory: a useful next basis design should target a margin-aware objective directly. Two candidate replacements:

    • Top-$k$ attention-KL between the original and compressed attention distributions — a smooth surrogate that directly bounds the argmax-survival distribution.
    • Margin objective: maximise the expected margin $m(t^*, t)$ under quantisation noise — non-quadratic but tractable, and tied directly to Observation 9.1.

    Neither admits the closed form QPCA enjoys. But QPCA is now the proven baseline that any margin-aware basis must beat, and the $k\_\mathrm{mse}$ / $\mathrm{logit\_err}$ split shows the question is empirically well-posed.

    A general lesson

    The derivation in §1–§7 is mathematically tight, and §8 confirms it: QPCA does exactly what the theory says. The disconnect is not a failure of the theory but a failure of the loss to capture what the application needs. Picking the right surrogate loss is half the problem; the closed-form derivation is the second half. This experiment is now a witness to the first half being underrated.

    References & closing remarks

    The derivation above is short but threads several deep ideas: oblique projection, congruence transforms, and the classical PCA optimality of Fan. The two references below are the load-bearing citations.

    References

    1. T. Linder, R. Zamir, and K. Zeger. "High-resolution source coding for non-difference distortion measures: Multidimensional companding." IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, vol. 45, no. 2, pp. 548–561, March 1999.
    2. K. Fan. "On a theorem of Weyl concerning eigenvalues of linear transformations I." Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, vol. 35, no. 11, pp. 652–655, 1949.

    Synopsis

    We posed the problem of compressing a set of keys $\{k_j\}$ to low-dimensional codes $\{r_j\}$ while preserving every inner product with a fixed query set $\{q_i\}$. The closed-form solution exploits two structural facts:

    (i) the query distribution enters the loss only through its second-moment matrix $M_q$, turning the all-pairs problem into a single $M_q$-weighted regression; (ii) the maximisation over the decoder, after eliminating the codes, becomes a standard PCA problem after rescaling by $M_q^{1/2}$.

    The optimal codec $$U^* = M_q^{-1/2} V_p, \qquad r_j^* = V_p^\top M_q^{1/2} k_j$$ where $V_p$ are the top-$p$ eigenvectors of $A = M_q^{1/2} S_k M_q^{1/2}$, has a striking quantisation property (Remark 6.4): the $M_q$-weighted reconstruction error in key space equals the plain Euclidean error in code space. Bit-allocation in the code space is therefore optimal with no further query weighting.

    Closing remark

    The derivation showcases a recurring pattern in applied linear algebra: a non-convex optimisation in an awkward coordinate system becomes convex (and classical) after the right change of variables. The change here is the spectral square root — the same transform that converts a Mahalanobis geometry into a Euclidean one. Recognising when such a substitution is available is, perhaps, the most transferable lesson.
