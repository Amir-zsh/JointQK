    Empirical Report · 2026-05-21

    K-Cache Quantization on Qwen3-8B

    TurboQuant · JointQK · QPCA — methods, calibration, results

    TL;DR

    We compare three K-cache quantization bases on Qwen/Qwen3-8B at $b \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ bits/coord, with V-side held fixed (Section 5):

    • TurboQuant — random Hadamard rotation, no calibration.
    • JointQK — the deployed production basis; eigenvectors of $\tfrac{1}{2}(\Sigma_Q \Sigma_K + \Sigma_K \Sigma_Q)$ with water-fill score $q\text{-diag}_i \cdot k\text{-diag}_i$.
    • QPCA — the closed-form minimiser of all-pairs Q-weighted logit MSE; basis $\Sigma_Q^{1/2} V$ with water-fill score $\lambda_i$.

    Result. QPCA wins decisively on the metric it was designed to minimise (logit_err, −16 % to −36 % vs JointQK) but loses on downstream LongBench F1: at $b = 2$, JointQK reaches 47.0 mean F1 to QPCA's 41.7 (−5.4 pp). At $b \in \{3, 4\}$, calibration-free TurboQuant beats both calibrated methods on mean F1.

    Why. Both calibrated methods (approximately) target the same Q-weighted logit MSE, an averaged objective. The bench metric responds to softmax-distribution shift, which is a per-pair threshold-flavoured quantity. The gap between these two is the entire phenomenon — see §8 for the mechanism analysis and Proposition 8.4 for a sketch of a more accurate objective (softmax KL) for the next-generation calibrated basis.

    Section 1

    Attention

    Self-attention scores each (query, key) pair with an inner product, normalises via softmax, and outputs a probability-weighted sum of values. The K-cache stores these keys; quantizing them changes the scores and therefore the output.

    Definition 1.1 — Scaled dot-product attention

    For a query $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a set of $S$ keys $\{k_s\}$ and values $\{v_s\}$ (one head): $$\ell_s \;=\; \frac{q^\top k_s}{\sqrt{d}}, \qquad \alpha_s \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}_s(\ell_s) \;=\; \frac{\exp(\ell_s)}{\sum_{s'} \exp(\ell_{s'})}, \qquad \mathrm{Attn}(q) \;=\; \sum_s \alpha_s\, v_s.$$ We call $\ell_s$ the logit of key $s$, and $\alpha_s$ the attention probability.

    Softmax exponentiates differences: $\alpha_{s_1}/\alpha_{s_2} = \exp(\ell_{s_1} - \ell_{s_2})$. A logit margin of 5 makes the top-1 about $e^5 \approx 150\times$ more probable than the runner-up. In transformer inference, attention distributions are typically peaky — one or a few keys dominate the output.

    The K-cache holds $\{k_s\}_{s=1}^{S}$ per (layer, KV-head); at long context $S$ is the dominant memory cost. Compressing the K-cache means storing each $k_s$ at fewer bits, replacing $k_s$ with a reconstruction $\hat k_s$ in the score formula.

    Proposition 1.2 — What quantization changes; how we measure the change

    Quantization replaces $k_s$ with $\hat k_s$, perturbing the logits $\ell_s \to \hat\ell_s$ and therefore the softmax weights $\alpha_s \to \hat\alpha_s$. The attention output is the softmax-weighted sum $\mathrm{Attn}(q) = \sum_s \alpha_s v_s$, so what we ultimately want preserved is the full softmax output distribution $\hat\alpha \approx \alpha$.

    That distributional question is hard to summarise in a single scalar. We measure two cheap discrete proxies instead:

    • top-1 retention: whether $\arg\max_s \hat\ell_s = \arg\max_s \ell_s$.
    • top-5 retention: whether the original argmax remains in the top-5 of $\hat\ell$.

    These proxies are neither necessary nor sufficient for output preservation. An argmax flip with similar value vectors barely moves the output; an argmax preservation with shifted softmax weights still moves it. We use them because (a) they are cheap (argmax comparisons over the sequence), and (b) prior empirical work on this model [4] found top-1 retention is the cheap proxy that correlates most strongly with downstream F1 — better than $k\_\text{mse}$ and other aggregate metrics. They are empirically-validated proxies in our setting, not logical consequences of softmax mechanics.

    We state the precise condition under which the top-1 proxy is satisfied; it will be useful in §8:

    Theorem 1.3 — Condition for top-1 retention

    Let $t^* := \arg\max_s \ell_s$ and $\Delta\ell_s := q^\top (\hat k_s - k_s) / \sqrt{d}$ be the per-key logit perturbation. Then $\arg\max_s \hat\ell_s = t^*$ iff for every $s \neq t^*$, $$\Delta\ell_{t^*} - \Delta\ell_s \;>\; -(\ell_{t^*} - \ell_s) \;=\; -m_s.$$ That is, the per-key perturbation gap must stay below the per-key margin $m_s$. Top-1 retention is a threshold statistic on per-key margins, not a function of any aggregate (e.g., MSE) over $s$.

    Section 2

    Compression framework: linear transform + per-coordinate quantization

    All three methods studied here share the same pipeline. They differ only in (i) the basis and (ii) how the bit budget is split across coordinates.

    Definition 2.1 — Quantization pipeline

    Fix an encoder matrix $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, decoder $G$ with $FG = I$, per-coord bit budgets $B = (B_1, \ldots, B_d)$ with $\sum_i B_i = b \cdot d$, and per-coord Lloyd–Max codebooks $\{\mathcal{C}_i\}_{i=1}^d$ matched to the marginal distribution of coordinate $i$ in code space.

    Encode a key (as row vector): $r \;=\; k \cdot F \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
    Quantize each coordinate independently: $\hat r_i \;=\; \arg\min_{c \in \mathcal{C}_i} (r_i - c)^2$.
    Decode: $\hat k \;=\; \hat r \cdot G$.

    Assumption 2.A — Codebooks are designed for Gaussian sources

    The per-coord codebooks $\mathcal{C}_i$ used in this report are Lloyd–Max codebooks for the unit-Gaussian distribution, scaled by the per-coord standard deviation $\sigma_i$. Concretely, the implementation calls unit_gaussian_centroids(B_i) and multiplies by $\sigma_i$. This is the standard high-resolution choice: it is optimal when $r_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)$ and remains close to optimal for any smooth, single-mode source after the basis rotation makes per-coord marginals approximately Gaussian (a consequence of central limit theorem mixing for $d \gg 1$). All $D(B) \approx c \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot 2^{-2 B}$ formulas in this section presume this Gaussian-Lloyd–Max codebook design; mismatch between the actual source and the Gaussian assumption shows up as a larger constant $c$, not as a change in the $2^{-2B}$ slope.

    Lloyd–Max with per-coordinate standard deviation matched to the source minimises per-coord scalar MSE. The per-coord bit budget $B_i$ controls codebook size $|\mathcal{C}_i| = 2^{B_i}$. We cap $B_i \le 8$ and redistribute saved bits to the lowest-allocated coords.

    Three design knobs distinguish methods:

    1. Basis — the matrix $F$.
    2. Bit allocation — how to choose $B$ given $b \cdot d$ total.
    3. Per-coord standard deviation — what scale to use for the Lloyd–Max codebooks.

    The codebook (knob 3) follows automatically from the variance of $r_i$ once the basis is chosen, so the interesting choices are (1) and (2).

    What scalar quantizers leave behind

    A $B$-bit scalar quantizer with $K = 2^B$ levels partitions the real line into $K$ cells and replaces every source sample with its cell's centroid. The error is the deviation within a cell. We need to know how this error scales with $B$ and with the source statistics — that scaling is what makes one bit allocation better than another.

    Proposition 2.2 — Bennett–Lloyd high-resolution formula

    For a scalar source with density $p(x)$ and finite second moment $\sigma^2$, an optimal $B$-bit scalar quantizer (Lloyd–Max with centroids placed to minimise MSE) achieves mean squared error $$D(B) \;\approx\; c \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot 2^{-2 B}, \qquad B \text{ large,}$$ where $c$ is a constant depending on $p$ (e.g., $c = \frac{\pi \sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 2.72$ for a Gaussian source). The approximation is asymptotically exact as $B \to \infty$ and is accurate to within ~20 % for Gaussian sources already at $B \ge 2$.

    The 6 dB / bit slope $2^{-2B}$ is the universal high-resolution scaling: each additional bit of precision cuts the per-coord MSE by a factor of 4. The proportionality constant absorbs source shape (Gaussian vs heavy-tailed) and codebook design (Lloyd–Max vs uniform).

    Remark 2.3 — What "high-resolution" means

    "High-resolution" is the asymptotic regime where the source density $p(x)$ is approximately constant inside each quantization cell — i.e., the cells are small relative to the scale of $p$. Practically: $B \ge 2$ for Gaussian sources. Below this, the Bennett–Lloyd formula picks up higher-order corrections, but its qualitative prediction (MSE $\propto \sigma^2 \cdot 2^{-2B}$) still holds. The K bit budgets we sweep ($b \in \{2, 3, 4\}$) are in the regime where the formula is accurate enough to guide allocation.

    Why we minimise $\sum_i s_i \cdot 2^{-2 B_i}$

    With one Lloyd–Max quantizer per coordinate, the total per-key MSE after compression decomposes as the sum of per-coordinate MSEs: $$\mathbb{E}\,\|r - \hat r\|^2 \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} \mathbb{E}\,(r_i - \hat r_i)^2 \;\approx\; c \sum_{i=1}^{d} \sigma_i^2 \cdot 2^{-2 B_i},$$ using Proposition 2.2 per coord with $\sigma_i^2 = \mathrm{Var}(r_i)$. Different design objectives reweight the coord contributions — e.g., a Q-weighted MSE may rescale by $\sigma_i^2 \to s_i$ where $s_i$ is the per-coord loss-weight. The water-fill problem is then a single allocation rule over all such weighted-MSE objectives:

    Lemma 2.4 — Water-fill bit allocation

    Given per-coord scores $s_i > 0$ and total budget $B_{\text{tot}} = b \cdot d$, the bit allocation that minimises $\sum_i s_i \cdot 2^{-2 B_i}$ subject to $\sum_i B_i = B_{\text{tot}}$ is $$B_i^* \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 s_i \;+\; \tfrac{B_{\text{tot}}}{d} \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2 d} \sum_j \log_2 s_j$$ (after clamping $B_i^* \ge 0$ and re-solving). This is the classical reverse water-filling allocation. Coordinates with larger score get more bits, on a $\frac{1}{2} \log_2$ scale.

    Equalisation property. At the optimum, the per-coord contribution to the loss is constant across all active coordinates: $$s_i \cdot 2^{-2 B_i^*} \;=\; C \qquad \text{for all } i \text{ with } B_i^* > 0,$$ for some constant $C$ (the "water level"). Geometrically: think of the score $s_i$ as the floor height $\frac{1}{2}\log_2 s_i$ of a bucket, and the bit allocation $B_i^*$ as the depth of water poured into that bucket. Water-fill keeps the water surface flat across all buckets. What this means in practice depends on what the score $s_i$ represents — see Proposition 8.2.

    Each method below defines a basis $F$ and a per-coord score $s_i$. The score equals the per-coord variance $\lambda_i$ for objectives that minimise code-space MSE (QPCA's choice), or a product of per-coord variance and per-coord query weight for objectives that approximate logit variance (JointQK's choice). Bit allocation then follows from Lemma 2.4.

    Section 3

    TurboQuant, JointQK, QPCA

    The three methods span the calibration-dependence spectrum: TurboQuant uses no calibration data, JointQK and QPCA both use the same calibration second moments, differing in how they use them.

    TurboQuant (V3)

    Name origin. Published as "TurboQuant" [3]; the "Turbo" refers to the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform implementation. Internally we label it V3 (the third K-side variant tested in early ablations).

    Method 3.1 — TurboQuant

    Basis: $F = H$, a fixed random Hadamard matrix (orthogonal, seeded for reproducibility); $G = H^\top$.
    Score: uniform $s_i = 1$ → uniform allocation $B_i = b$ for all $i$.
    Calibration: none.
    Rationale: random rotations approximately equalise the per-coord variance of any input distribution, so uniform allocation is near-optimal without knowing $S_k$.

    JointQK

    Name origin. "Joint" because the basis is built from both $\Sigma_Q$ and $\Sigma_K$ (vs. PCA-on-K which uses $\Sigma_K$ alone). The "QK" emphasises the symmetric use of query and key statistics.

    Method 3.2 — JointQK

    Basis: $F = R$ where $R$ is the orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetrised product $$\tfrac{1}{2}(\Sigma_Q \Sigma_K + \Sigma_K \Sigma_Q), \qquad R \text{ sorted by descending eigenvalue;}$$ $G = R^\top$.
    Score: per-coord product $$s_i^{\text{JointQK}} \;=\; (R^\top \Sigma_Q R)_{ii} \cdot (R^\top \Sigma_K R)_{ii} \;=\; q\text{-diag}_i \cdot k\text{-diag}_i.$$
    Calibration: uses $\Sigma_Q$, $\Sigma_K$ (Definition 4.1).
    Status: deployed production basis for Qwen3-8B.

    QPCA

    Name origin. "Q-weighted PCA" — like ordinary PCA on keys, but the inner product is $\Sigma_Q$-weighted (Mahalanobis) instead of Euclidean. When $\Sigma_Q = I$ the construction reduces to plain PCA. Closed-form derivation in qpca_derivation_companion.html.

    Method 3.3 — QPCA

    Let $A := \Sigma_Q^{1/2} \Sigma_K \Sigma_Q^{1/2} = V \Lambda V^\top$ with $V$ orthonormal and $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_d)$.
    Basis: $F = \Sigma_Q^{1/2} V$ (non-orthogonal in Euclidean sense; $\Sigma_Q$-orthonormal); $G = V^\top \Sigma_Q^{-1/2}$.
    Score: per-coord variance in code space, $s_i^{\text{QPCA}} = \lambda_i$.
    Calibration: uses $\Sigma_Q$, $\Sigma_K$.
    Property: QPCA is the closed-form minimiser of the all-pairs logit MSE $\sum_{i,j} (q_i^\top k_j - q_i^\top G^\top \! F^\top k_j)^2$ over linear codecs (companion derivation Theorem 6.1). Linder–Zamir–Zeger [1] additionally prove QPCA is optimal for high-resolution source coding under any smooth quadratic distortion measure.

    Section 4

    Calibration

    Both calibrated methods (JointQK, QPCA) read the same two second-moment matrices. They are computed offline, once per (layer, KV-head), from a held-out prompt corpus.

    Definition 4.1 — Calibration second moments

    Given a calibration corpus producing $N$ prefill tokens with per-(layer, KV-head) queries $\{q_n\}$ and keys $\{k_n\}$ (queries group-pooled across the Q-heads sharing each KV-head under grouped-query attention): $$\Sigma_Q \;:=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} q_n q_n^\top, \qquad \Sigma_K \;:=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} k_n k_n^\top, \qquad \Sigma_Q, \Sigma_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}.$$ These are uncentered second moments — the calibration uses them as estimators of the corresponding population moments under the calibration prompt distribution.

    This run's corpus: compact8

    400 LongBench train prompts (50 per task × 8 tasks: qasper, hotpotqa, musique, qmsum, multi_news, triviaqa, passage_retrieval_en, repobench-p), tokenised with the Qwen3 tokenizer; total ≈ 4.5 M prefill tokens after RoPE. Frozen seed (20260504), token-length filter [2 k, 32 k]. The same corpus and the same $(\Sigma_Q, \Sigma_K)$ tensors feed both JointQK and QPCA.

    Layer 0 keys exhibit anomalous attention-sink behaviour (a small set of position-0 tokens with extreme norms). For the headline metrics we exclude layer 0; for inference we keep layer 0 at full precision.

    Remark 4.2 — Calibration drift

    The basis quality depends on how well the calibration $\Sigma_Q, \Sigma_K$ estimate the evaluation-time distribution. With 4.5 M tokens across 8 tasks the estimates are stable across re-runs; the principal risk is task-distribution mismatch when the eval corpus includes tasks not in the calibration mix (we evaluate on 12 LongBench tasks including 4 multi-doc QA tasks absent from calibration — they remain in-distribution at the per-(L,h) second-moment level).

    Section 5

    Experimental setup

    To isolate the effect of the K-side basis, we fix V-side quantization across all comparisons. Three experiments at increasing fidelity to the production goal: K-fidelity proxies (pooled, then per-task) and downstream F1.

    Why we fix V quantization at this stage

    K-cache and V-cache quantization interact: errors on the V-side change the output value $\hat v_s$ linearly, weighted by the attention probability $\alpha_{t,s}$; errors on the K-side change $\alpha_{t,s}$ itself (through the softmax). The two effects compound. If we varied both K and V together, the F1 deltas would conflate K-basis choice with V-basis choice.

    We therefore hold V to the deployed-production V configuration in every cell of the comparison: random Hadamard rotation (calibration-free), uniform $B_i = 3$ bits/coord, layer 0 full precision. This is the V-side V_TURBOQUANT @ V=3 setting locked in artifacts/v_bases/v_lock.txt after the JointQK F1-inversion investigation [4].

    Model and eval

    • Model: Qwen/Qwen3-8B (36 layers, 32 Q-heads, 8 KV-heads, head_dim 128, GQA group 4). fp16 inference. Prefill-only K compression (decode keeps fp16).
    • Calibration: compact8 (Section 4), 400 train prompts.
    • Evaluation: 12 LongBench tasks at fraction=1.0; calibration train rows excluded from eval where they overlap (7 tasks).
    • Bit budgets: $b \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ K bits/coord. V at 3 bits/coord throughout.

    Section 6

    Metrics

    Two layers: K-fidelity proxies (cheap, computed from quantization noise alone) and downstream task F1 (expensive, requires full model inference and LongBench grading).

    K-fidelity proxies

    Definition 6.1 — Per-row metrics on a single (layer, KV-head)

    Given a held-out prompt with $T$ tokens, true keys $K = [k_1, \ldots, k_T]$ and reconstructions $\hat K$:

    • top-1 retention: $\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{1}\bigl[\arg\max_s (q_t^\top k_s) = \arg\max_s (q_t^\top \hat k_s)\bigr]$. Probability that the original top-1 attention argmax survives.
    • top-5 retention: same but checking the original argmax appears in the top-5 of the reconstructed scores.
    • k_mse: $\frac{1}{T \cdot d} \|K - \hat K\|_F^2$, unweighted reconstruction error.
    • logit_err: $\frac{1}{T^2} \sum_{t,s} (q_t^\top (k_s - \hat k_s))^2 = \frac{1}{T^2} \sum_s (k_s - \hat k_s)^\top \Sigma_Q^{(T)} (k_s - \hat k_s)$, the Q-weighted reconstruction error using the per-prompt query second moment $\Sigma_Q^{(T)} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t q_t q_t^\top$. This is the empirical estimator of the QPCA objective.

    Headline numbers are means over (layers ≥ 1) × (KV-heads) × (test prompts), with layer 0 excluded.

    What the proxies actually proxy

    The K quantization output enters the model through the softmax: $\hat\alpha_s = \mathrm{softmax}_s(q^\top \hat k_s / \sqrt{d})$. What we ultimately want preserved is $\hat\alpha \approx \alpha$, since the attention output is the $\hat\alpha$-weighted sum of values. top-1 / top-5 retention are discrete proxies for that distributional question (Proposition 1.2); they are accurate when softmax is peaky and looser when softmax is flat. k_mse and logit_err are aggregate (summed) proxies — they characterise how much the logits move on average, but say nothing about whether the largest logits' relative order is preserved.

    Downstream F1

    Standard LongBench task-specific scoring [5]: token-level F1 against gold answers for QA tasks; ROUGE-based F1 for summarisation; exact-match accuracy for classification (trec, samsum). Reported on each task at fraction=1.0 with calibration train rows excluded from eval where applicable.

    The downstream F1 is the metric production cares about. The K-fidelity proxies are useful because each cell is ~10× cheaper than the full F1 sweep — they let us rank candidate K-side bases before committing GPU time. Whether the ranking transfers is itself an empirical question, and is addressed in §7.

    Section 8

    Discussion: why QPCA is not the best choice

    QPCA wins the metric it minimises (logit_err) and loses the metric production cares about (downstream F1). The math explains the gap; we summarise the mechanism.

    QPCA targets the wrong loss

    QPCA is the closed-form minimiser of $\sum_{i,j} (q_i^\top k_j - q_i^\top G^\top F^\top k_j)^2$, the all-pairs squared logit error. Aggregated across all (query, key) pairs, this is the empirical estimator of logit_err — exactly the metric on which QPCA wins by 16–36 % in Table 7.1.

    But the bench metric is not logit MSE. It is downstream F1, which is mediated by the softmax-weighted attention output $\sum_s \hat\alpha_s v_s$. F1 changes when the softmax distribution $\hat\alpha$ shifts meaningfully, and that distribution depends on where the logit perturbations land — not just on their aggregate magnitude.

    We don't measure softmax-distribution shift directly. We measure two discrete proxies: top-1 and top-5 retention. These are not logical consequences of softmax mechanics — they are empirical proxies, validated on this model by [4] as the cheap summaries that correlate most strongly with F1 across calibrated and uncalibrated K bases. Theorem 1.3 says top-1 retention is a threshold statistic on per-key margins; aggregate MSE is blind to that structure. Two K reconstructions with identical $k\_\text{mse}$ or $\text{logit\_err}$ can have very different top-1 retentions if their per-key noise concentrates differently relative to the per-key margins.

    Principle 8.1 — Per-key margins matter; aggregate noise does not

    The top-1 retention proxy (Theorem 1.3) is determined by per-key margin-to-noise comparisons: each runner-up $s$ survives quantization unflipped iff its perturbation gap stays below its margin $m_s$. Aggregate metrics like $k\_\text{mse}$ and $\text{logit\_err}$ sum noise across all $s$ without distinguishing close races (small $m_s$) from blowouts (large $m_s$). A method that puts its precision budget on the directions that drive small-margin logits will preserve more argmaxes than a method with the same total noise that spreads the budget without regard to margin structure.

    Concretely: noise on a key $s$ with $m_s = 5$ rarely flips its order (a 5-unit perturbation is needed). Noise on a key $s$ with $m_s = 0.3$ flips its order easily. The two methods we compare have the same total quantization budget but differ in which coordinates they protect — and therefore in how much of their noise lands on small-margin vs large-margin keys.

    Two parameterizations of the same objective

    Both methods target the same Q-weighted total logit MSE $$L \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q,k}\bigl[(q^\top(k - \hat k))^2\bigr] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_k\bigl[(k - \hat k)^\top \Sigma_Q (k - \hat k)\bigr].$$ They differ in how they parameterise the minimisation.

    Proposition 8.2 — QPCA is plain PCA in the $\Sigma_Q$-rescaled space

    Define $\tilde k := \Sigma_Q^{1/2} k$ and $\tilde q := \Sigma_Q^{-1/2} q$. The logit is unchanged: $\tilde q^\top \tilde k = q^\top k$. In the rescaled space:

    • $\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde k) \;=\; \Sigma_Q^{1/2} \Sigma_K \Sigma_Q^{1/2} \;=:\; A$;
    • $\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde q) \;=\; \Sigma_Q^{-1/2} \Sigma_Q \Sigma_Q^{-1/2} \;=\; I$;
    • $L \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\tilde k}[\|\tilde k - \hat{\tilde k}\|^2]$ — the Q-weighted key MSE becomes plain Euclidean MSE on rescaled keys.

    QPCA's encoder $r = V^\top \tilde k$ with $V$ = eigenvectors of $A$ is therefore plain PCA on $\tilde k$, and its score $s_i = \lambda_i$ is the variance of $\tilde k$ along eigenvector $i$. Note that $\lambda_i$ is not query-blind — the rescaling $\Sigma_Q^{1/2}$ folded the query covariance into the keys, so $\lambda_i$ encodes both $\Sigma_K$ and $\Sigma_Q$. QPCA uses query information through this fold-in; it is just packaged inside $A$ rather than appearing as a separate factor in the score.

    JointQK parameterises the same objective differently. In its orthogonal basis $R$ (eigenvectors of $\tfrac{1}{2}(\Sigma_Q \Sigma_K + \Sigma_K \Sigma_Q)$ in the original Euclidean space), $$L \;\approx\; \sum_i \underbrace{(R^\top \Sigma_Q R)_{ii}}_{q\text{-diag}_i} \cdot \underbrace{(R^\top \Sigma_K R)_{ii}}_{k\text{-diag}_i} \cdot 2^{-2 B_i} \cdot c,$$ under an additional independence approximation (the per-coord errors in $R$'s basis are uncorrelated with the per-coord query coefficients). JointQK water-fills against this separable score $s_i = q\text{-diag}_i \cdot k\text{-diag}_i$. This is an approximation to $L$, not its exact value.

    So QPCA wins on the proxy that estimates $L$ directly (logit_err) by 16–36 % — confirming the math: QPCA's parameterisation is exact, JointQK's is a separable approximation.

    The unresolved bit. The bench metric is F1, not $L$. $L$ averages the logit error over the calibration query distribution $\Sigma_Q$; argmax retention depends on specific per-$(q, s)$ margins (Theorem 1.3) and per-pair noise structure, which $L$ marginalises out. The fact that JointQK's approximation wins on F1 while losing on $L$ means JointQK's approximation error is in a direction more favourable to per-pair argmax preservation than QPCA's exact answer is. We do not have a clean mechanistic explanation of why this happens on this model — it is an empirical observation about the gap between an averaged objective ($L$) and a per-pair threshold metric (argmax). A clean follow-up experiment would swap allocations across bases (JointQK basis with QPCA's $\lambda_i$ score; QPCA basis with JointQK's separable score) to isolate which choice carries the F1 effect.

    The k_mse–logit_err split confirms the mechanism

    In Table 7.1 both methods are essentially tied on k_mse (unweighted reconstruction error, which neither targets), while QPCA wins logit_err by 16–36 % (which QPCA minimises by construction). This is the signature of the design objective being correctly minimised. QPCA does exactly what its theory predicts; the loss it minimises is the wrong loss for the downstream metric.

    The Linder–Zamir–Zeger result [1] establishes QPCA as optimal for high-resolution source coding under any smooth quadratic distortion. Argmax preservation is not a quadratic distortion — it is a 0/1 threshold — and the LZZ theorem does not extend to it. Even at $b = 4$, where MSE drops by ~30× relative to $b = 2$, ~14 % of attention argmaxes still flip. The threshold metric does not smooth out with bits.

    A more accurate objective: softmax KL

    Both QPCA and JointQK approximately target Q-weighted logit MSE, which weights per-key squared errors uniformly across the sequence. A loss that more closely tracks output preservation should up-weight the keys that actually carry attention mass and down-weight the rest. The natural such loss is the KL divergence between the original and compressed softmax attention distributions.

    Proposition 8.4 — Softmax KL is attention-weighted logit variance (high-resolution)

    For a query $q$ with original logits $\ell$ and perturbed logits $\hat\ell = \ell + \Delta\ell$, let $\alpha_s = \mathrm{softmax}_s(\ell)$ and $\hat\alpha_s = \mathrm{softmax}_s(\hat\ell)$. Then $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\alpha \,\Vert\, \hat\alpha) \;=\; \sum_s \alpha_s \log\frac{\alpha_s}{\hat\alpha_s}.$$ Expanding to second order in $\Delta\ell$, $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\alpha \,\Vert\, \hat\alpha) \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_\alpha(\Delta\ell) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\sum_s \alpha_s (\Delta\ell_s)^2 \;-\; \Bigl(\sum_s \alpha_s \Delta\ell_s\Bigr)^{\!\!2}\right].$$

    Proof

    $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\alpha \,\Vert\, \hat\alpha) = -\langle \Delta\ell \rangle_\alpha + \log(\hat Z / Z)$ where $Z = \sum_s e^{\ell_s}$ and $\hat Z / Z = \sum_s \alpha_s e^{\Delta\ell_s}$. Expanding $e^{\Delta\ell_s} \approx 1 + \Delta\ell_s + \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta\ell_s)^2$ and $\log(1 + x) \approx x - \tfrac{1}{2} x^2$: $\log(\hat Z / Z) \approx \langle \Delta\ell \rangle_\alpha + \tfrac{1}{2}[\langle \Delta\ell^2 \rangle_\alpha - \langle \Delta\ell \rangle_\alpha^2]$. Substitute: the $\langle \Delta\ell \rangle_\alpha$ terms cancel. $\square$

    Compare with the QPCA / JointQK objective $L = \mathbb{E}_q\!\sum_s (\Delta\ell_s)^2$. Two structural differences:

    • Per-key attention weighting. Softmax KL weights the per-key error by $\alpha_s$: high-$\alpha$ keys (the output drivers) are protected; near-zero-$\alpha$ keys are effectively ignored. $L$ weights every key uniformly.
    • Logit-shift invariance. Softmax KL subtracts the mean perturbation $\langle \Delta\ell \rangle_\alpha$ before squaring — a constant shift in all logits does not change the softmax, so the KL ignores it. $L$ does not.

    Softmax KL is what F1 actually responds to (up to the per-task scoring on the model's downstream output). It is the right objective for an argmax-aware K-cache basis. The cost is that $\alpha_s$ depends on the specific query $q$ and on the original logits $\ell$ — so no closed-form basis-and-allocation solution exists in the QPCA style. A method targeting softmax KL would need either iterative optimisation, or a tractable approximation (e.g. estimating $\langle \alpha_s \rangle$ from calibration data and using a fixed attention-prior weighting in place of the per-query $\alpha_s$). This is the direction we believe the next-generation calibrated K bases should explore.
