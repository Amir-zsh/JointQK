0Protocol — shared settings for every experiment

Serving stack. Every cell, every method, both models: the same engine, token pool 140,000 per GPU, CUDA graphs, up to 24 concurrent requests. All quantized methods share OSCAR's mixed-KV window policy — the first 64 tokens (attention sinks) and the most recent 256 tokens stay in bf16; everything older is quantized as it ages out of the ring. Clip ratios are the OSCAR defaults, K 0.96 / V 0.92 (Naive and QuaRot run clip 1.0). So a "vq2 vs int2" contrast differs only in how aged K is stored — same window, same INT2-V path, same rotations. There is no sequence cap below the pool; the longest requests are NIAH-64K (65,536-token prompt + 128 generated).

NIAH (both models, all methods). Contexts 8K / 16K / 32K / 64K; 800 rows per context = 8 RULER subtasks × 100 (single_1–3, multikey_1–3, multivalue, multiquery). Greedy decoding, exactly 1 rollout per row, generation cap 128 tokens. Accuracy = RULER string_match per subtask; the headline number is the unweighted mean over the 8 subtasks.

Llama reasoning/code. GPQA-diamond 198 / math500 full 500 / AIME25 30 / HumanEval 164 rows. 5 rollouts per prompt, T=0.6 / top-p 0.9 / no top-k (Llama-3.1 generation_config), non-thinking; generation caps gpqa+math 4096, aime 8192, humaneval 2048. 4Mechanism: why VQ-V loses served despite 2× better offline MSE

Section 3 leaves a contradiction. The offline de-risk said VQ-V reconstructs V at roughly half the error of scalar INT2-V — yet served on NIAH, it scores worse. Both measurements are correct, so something about the offline setup must not match what serving actually stresses. We had three candidate explanations and ran one targeted check for each.

Shared setup for all three checks: V captured live from Qwen3-8B prefill, rotated by the same Rv the engine loads, permuted the way the engine stores it, layer-0 excluded per repo convention. Script: pipelines/oscar_e2e/vqv_e5m2_check.py (plus inline variants for checks 2 and 3).

Check 1: does the fp8 centroid snap eat the advantage? REFUTED

The engine cannot store fp16 centroids — the sm80 bitcast constraint forces them into fp8-e5m2. Your offline de-risk used fp16 centroids, so the served path quantizes slightly coarser than the path that was measured. If that snap costs enough precision, the 2× advantage could vanish before the engine ever runs. We reran the comparison with the engine's exact e5m2-snapped centroids, assignments included:

methodrel-MSEvs INT2-V
INT2-V (per-token affine, clip 0.92)0.124—
VQ-V, fp16 centroids (the de-risk config)0.0580.47×
VQ-V, e5m2 centroids (what the engine runs)0.0600.48×

The snap costs +3.3% — VQ-V keeps essentially the full 2× advantage exactly as the engine executes it (and the fp16 row reproduces your 0.49× de-risk number). Not the explanation.

Check 2: is the average hiding catastrophic tokens? REFUTED

Average MSE can look great while a handful of tokens reconstruct terribly — and needle retrieval depends on the few tokens that carry the answer, not on the average. If VQ-V had a bad tail (tokens far from every codeword), that would reconcile good-average with bad-retrieval. We compared the full per-token error distributions instead of the mean. VQ-V wins at every quantile — median, p99, p999, and the single worst token. Its worst token (relMSE 0.12–0.14 by layer) reconstructs better than INT2-V's median token (≈0.125). There is no tail to blame.

Check 3: does the advantage survive on NIAH text? CONFIRMED

Both prior checks — and the original de-risk — measured V from GPQA prompts, which is also the text the codebook was trained on. But NIAH serves haystack text: repeated filler sentences, essay prose, dense blocks of random digits and uuids. The two methods should react very differently to that shift. INT2-V has no training data at all — its per-token affine scale adapts to whatever token it sees. A codebook is frozen at calibration time. So we repeated the measurement on V captured from actual NIAH-haystack rows:

V sourceINT2-VVQ-V (e5m2)ratio
GPQA prompts (= calibration domain)0.1240.0600.48×
NIAH haystack (= served regime)0.126–0.1300.097–0.1000.76–0.80×

Exactly the predicted asymmetry: INT2-V doesn't move (0.124 → ~0.128), while the codebook's error grows +65% out of domain, cutting its advantage roughly in half. This is the mechanism.

Conclusion. The served deficit is codebook domain sensitivity — the same property we measured on the K side (your codebook vs ours, 8–12 NIAH pts) — with one crucial difference: K errors pass through a softmax that forgives them, while V errors add linearly to the attention output. So the out-of-domain penalty on V converts directly into retrieval accuracy. The subtask pattern agrees: the deficit concentrates in multikey_2/3, whose haystacks are dense random-digit/uuid key-value text — the furthest thing from GPQA prose in the whole benchmark. One honest residual: even on NIAH-domain V the codebook's average MSE is still nominally below INT2-V's, yet it loses served — the remaining gap is likely error concentrated on needle tokens specifically (an attention-weighted rather than uniform error measure would resolve it). Constructive next step either way: a mixed-domain V codebook is the lever most likely to flip the VQ-V ablation.

5Bugs found and fixed this run (both gated, tracker updated)

  1. VQ-V prefix dequant (handoff patch; fixed in clone 6f78cd6cd). dequantize_prefix_kv decoded the V index arena as packed int2 crumbs during chunked prefill — garbage V for every prompt longer than one chunk, invisible to all single-chunk smokes and to gates G4/G5 (a third reader of the format, never updated). Fixed + new gate G6. Full write-up: artifact 02c62657.
  2. merge_shards scoring (ours). CSV round-trip stringified NIAH answer lists; the ruler scorer then matched characters ("['1679215']" → 7/11 → 63.64%). All sharded NIAH cells were mis-scored on first merge (bf16-32K read 71.4; true 97.4). Caught by a physical-impossibility check (baselines "improving" with context) before anything was recorded; fixed (literal_eval before scoring) and re-merged.
  1. TurboQuant baseline: unimplemented-as-documented, then mis-tuned (engine; fixed in clone commits a9a4ab6d3 + b04860e71). The documented recipe — SGLANG_LLOYD_MAX=1 on the int2plain path — pointed at a flag that only the mixed OSCAR pool read; the plain pool serving QuaRot/Naive/TurboQuant would have silently run min-max, i.e., QuaRot mislabeled as TurboQuant. On top of that, the legacy Lloyd-Max encoding (LM_RATIO=1.16, fitted to match the old clip path's dynamic range) costs +26% distortion vs exact Lloyd-Max. The fix adds the LM branch to both plain-pool writers — the fused-Hadamard decode writer and the pre-rotated prefill writer (the same two-writer bug class as item 1) — storing the MSE-optimal uniform-level encoding Δ* = 0.98774: +1.2% vs exact LM analytically, +0.8% on real post-FWHT Llama K/V (turbo_lm_check.py). Readers keep the plain (q−zero)·scale contract, which sidesteps the exact-centroid dequant path's documented decode instability. Gates verify_turbo_lm.py T1–T4 all pass (kernel-vs-torch parity 100%, writer-vs-writer arena parity 100%). Served family control: a 600-token greedy decode loops under every plain-int2 method (tail uniqueness: turbo 0.15, QuaRot 0.10, bf16 0.62; no CJK-spam signature) — looping is the no-bf16-band family behavior, not an LM defect.

Also fixed en route: a pool-runner stale-bootlog race (readiness grep matched the previous server's banner).

Heads-up for your tree. torch.save of a strided view persists the full underlying storage: the ::stride pool subsampling in capture_gpqa_concat.py writes each example at 4× its logical size (33 GB per pool). Our variant adds .contiguous() before saving; the same one-line fix applies to yours.
