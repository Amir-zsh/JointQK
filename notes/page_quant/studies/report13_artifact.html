The 64k-calibrated codebook does not degrade at 2× its calibrated positions — it collapses to zero: keys at unseen RoPE positions encode to garbage codewords and retrieval dies wholesale. The 128k-calibrated codebook, identical in every other respect, serves 128K at the usual ~7-pt distance below bf16 — the same context-flat gap seen at every other length.

Design rule. Codebook calibration must cover the entire serving position range. It costs nothing — same corpus, same token budget, just longer concat sequences — and the under-coverage failure mode is not graceful.
Cross-check: Samuel's Qwen 128K result looks different — it isn't. The difference is YaRN.

Samuel's numbers at 128K (Qwen3-8B, 80 rows): bf16 83.4, vq2 76.6 (−6.8), OSCAR-INT2 25.9. At first glance this contradicts our cliff — his vq2 uses the same 64K-calibrated gpqacc64k codebook, yet scores 76.6 where ours scored 0.16. The reconciliation is in how each model reaches 128K.

Background — two ways to serve long context. Every RoPE model is trained on a bounded position range, and each position is encoded as a set of rotation angles applied to Q and K. Qwen3-8B's trained range is 32,768 positions; to serve 128K, Samuel enables YaRN (his server config: rope_type: yarn, factor 4.0), which rescales the rotation frequencies so that positions 0–131,072 are compressed into the angular range the model was trained on — position 128K gets roughly the angles that position 32K would have natively. Llama-3.1-8B needs no such trick: its trained range is already 131,072, so our run uses native RoPE — positions past 64K produce angle combinations that genuinely exist nowhere below 64K.

Why this decides the codebook's fate. A codebook quantizes post-RoPE keys, so what it must cover is the distribution of rotation angles it will meet at serving time — not the position numbers. Under YaRN ×4, every key up to 128K carries angles from the native 0–32K range, which a codebook calibrated on ≤64K sequences has seen thoroughly: it stays in-distribution, and vq2 shows its usual ~7-point gap below bf16 (−6.8, matching our −7.9 with the 128K-calibrated codebook). Under native RoPE, keys past 64K carry angles the 64K-calibrated codebook has never encountered: they snap to meaningless codewords and retrieval collapses to zero. Same law, observed from both sides — the cliff is governed by post-RoPE angular coverage, not by raw position index.

Falsification test proposed to Samuel: serve Qwen at 128K without YaRN (pure extrapolation). Our finding predicts his 64K codebook collapses there exactly as ours did. (His OSCAR-INT2 at 25.9 also fits: a scalar quantizer has no codebook to go out-of-distribution — it just continues its usual context decay, 23.4 at 64K → 25.9 at 128K.)

Notes: single_1 (repeated-sentence noise haystack) collapses for everyone at 128K including bf16 (6.0) — a base-model failure on degenerate text at extreme length, not quantization (vq2_128k's 19 suggests quantization noise breaks the repetition attractor). The 131072 row set is Llama-tokenizer-budgeted (ctx 130048 + 1024 headroom), so no request exceeds native RoPE.

Model: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. Setup: 200-row stratified slice of niah_32768, identical rids across configurations. Theory 1: gen cap 512 vs 128 (bf16/int2/vq2). Theory 2: int2 with K-clip 1.0 vs 0.96, cap 128.

Diagnosis: the reasoning fine-tune leaves K distributions intrinsically harder to 2-bit-quantize (clip choice alone swings int2 by 14.5 points), and the deficit is real retrieval damage. The grid's growing vq2-over-int2 margins are genuine method differences — per-token-norm + codebook absorbs heavy tails better than affine min-max. Open directions: a tighter int2 clip sweep (0.92–0.94), and calibrating on R1's own generated text.
